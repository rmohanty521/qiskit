## The idea in one paragraph

Each cube has only **6 legal ways** to sit in the tower once you ignore which way round each face pair points: choose one opposite-face pair for front/back and a different one for left/right. Six options fit in **3 qubits**, so N cubes need **3N qubits** (12 for the four-cube puzzle, instead of the starter's 24). The rules become an energy (a Hamiltonian of Z operators) that is zero exactly for a solved tower. We start in an equal mix of the legal choices and run **constrained QAOA**: a cost layer that rewards towers obeying the rules, and a per-cube mixer that only ever swaps a legal choice for another legal choice, so no penalty terms are needed. Angles are tuned by minimising the **average cost**, never by using the answer. Every shot we report is decoded and checked with the starter's `solves`.

## Classical checks (all are `assert`s in the notebook)

1. The Hamiltonian equals the classical cost on **every** valid state for N = 4 to 6 (1,296 / 7,776 / 46,656 states) and on a seeded sample of 3,000 for N = 7 and 8. Largest gap: 0.
2. The zero-energy states are exactly the brute-force answers: 2 states = `count_answers` = the graph method, and both pass `solves(tower_of(...))`. Nothing wrong ties with them (next-lowest cost is 2).
3. An independent check: the starter's 24-qubit QUBO cost is zero on exactly the same 2 states.
4. The start state puts 1/6 on each valid code and 0 on codes 6 and 7.
5. At random angles the full circuit leaks about 1e-15 probability outside the valid codes; every measured run had **0 invalid-code shots**.
6. Our fast 6^N simulator (used for tuning) matches Qiskit's `Statevector` of the real circuit to about 1e-17.
7. The decoder is tested on a known answer (qubit 0 is the rightmost character of a Qiskit key).
8. **Every shot is decoded and checked with `solves`.** That fraction is the P(correct tower) below.

- **Random-guess baseline**: the chance that a uniform random bitstring of our encoding is a solved tower (2 / 8^N). We also give the chance for a uniform random *legal* choice (2 / 6^N), since our circuit only produces legal choices.
- Two-qubit gates and depth are counted after `transpile(qc, basis_gates=["rz", "sx", "x", "cx"])`.
- All qBraid job IDs are also in `results/jobs.json`. One extra 100-shot SV1 job (`aws:aws:sim:sv1-60f2-qjob-6ad3833038127b44c71b9a20`, a 2-qubit Bell state) was a connection test and is not part of the results.

**What it tells us.**
- For four cubes, success grows steadily with depth: from 1.7% at p = 1 to 73.3% at p = 12 on SV1, against 0.15% for a random legal guess. No shot was ever an illegal code.
- The SV1 shots agree with the exact simulation (shown in the notebook) within shot noise. The largest gap is N = 8: 66 solved shots of 2,000 (3.3%) against 4.3% expected (about 86 shots), a 2-standard-deviation dip.
- At a fixed depth (p = 10), success falls as N grows (exact: 58%, 9.1%, 9.5%, 3.5%, 4.3% for N = 4 to 8), but random guessing falls faster (a factor of 6 per cube), so the gain over guessing grows from about 375× to about 36,000×. Keeping success high at larger N would need more layers. We did not measure how many.
- Two-qubit gates per layer grow from about 550 (N = 4) to about 2,100 (N = 8). These circuits are fine on a simulator but far too deep for today's hardware.
- **No speedup claim.** Brute force checks all 331,776 four-cube towers in about 0.14 s, and tuning the angles needed a full classical simulation of the state.

**Next thing we would try:** measure how many layers keep success near 50% as N grows, using a CVaR objective and angles transferred from smaller N.

## AI use note

We used **Claude Code** (Anthropic) as a coding agent throughout. It proposed and wrote the compact encoding, the Hamiltonian builder, the constrained mixer, the exact 6^N simulator, the angle tuning, the notebook (`build_notebook.py`). We decided the approach and reviewed the code.

How we kept it correct:
- **Model vs brute force:** the Hamiltonian is compared with the classical cost on every valid state (or a large seeded sample for N = 7, 8). Its zero-energy states are compared with `count_answers` and with an independent graph-method enumeration.
- **Independent cost:** the starter's own QUBO cost is checked to give the same answers.
- **Simulator vs Qiskit:** the fast simulator used for tuning is checked against `Statevector` of the actual circuit.
- **Decoder:** tested on a known answer, given Qiskit's bit order.
- **Every reported tower** passes the starter's `solves`, and angles are tuned on average cost only, never on the answer.
- No API keys are in the code. `QbraidProvider()` reads the credentials from the environment.
